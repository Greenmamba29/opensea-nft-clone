## Annie Turnbo Malone (1869–1957) — *Scale the people, not just the product*

**The stat:** Founded Poro College in St. Louis in **1918** — a beauty-industry training
institution that created **tens of thousands of jobs** for Black women, and
mentored/employed Sarah Breedlove before she became Madam C.J. Walker.
*(sources: Missouri Historical Society; Smithsonian NMAAHC collections)*

**Why it's on the slide:** A century before "platform economics," Malone built a
training-and-distribution engine that turned customers into earners. GrahmOS's
merchant-enablement model follows the same playbook: the platform's value is the
workforce it creates, not just the storefront it rents.

## Greenwood District, Tulsa — "Black Wall Street" — *The mall thesis, proven in 1921*

**The stat:** By the early 1900s, Greenwood was a self-contained commercial ecosystem of
**dozens of Black-owned businesses** — groceries, hotels (incl. the Stradford Hotel),
theaters, and professional offices — built on land developments by O.W. Gurley, where
dollars recirculated within the community. Destroyed in the Tulsa Race Massacre
(May 31–June 1, 1921), it was **partially rebuilt by its own community** in the years
that followed. *(sources: Oklahoma Commission to Study the Tulsa Race Riot of 1921,
2001 report; Greenwood Cultural Center; Tulsa Historical Society)*

**Why it's on the slide:** Greenwood is the closest documented ancestor of the GrahmOS
virtual mall — proof that a dense, community-owned commercial district functions as a
wealth engine. GrahmOS rebuilds that ecosystem in software, without the geographic
fragility.
